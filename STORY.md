# The Testament of the Archsage

*The overarching plot of* The Dreamlands, *told in the manner of H.P. Lovecraft.*

> "…the harbour of Serannian, the pink marble city of the clouds, which is built on that ethereal coast where the west wind flows into the sky…"
> — H.P. Lovecraft, *Celephaïs* (1920)

## Preface

I who write this was once called by another name, in a temple whose stones the sea has since reclaimed; and the name by which the valley knows me now—the Archsage—was given me by people who did not know what I had been.[^2](ANNOTATIONS.md#a2) I set these pages down in my tower above the Drowned Coast, where the grey swell breaks all night upon reefs older than any shore, because my ward is thinning as my strength thins, and because it is fitting that some sober record should survive of what my pupil did before the minstrels make of it a thing of gilt and trumpets. I do not ask to be believed. I ask only that whoever reads this look up on some clear night above the Miskatonic, and count the stars, and remember that there was a season when there were fewer of them; and that it was a chandler's helper out of Arkham who gave them back.

## Part I: The Waking World

### I. Of the Thing I Served

I was born on a reef-isle of the Drowned Coast, among fisher-folk who drew up in their nets, with the cod and the weed, green-crusted fragments of carven stone that no hand of our people had cut.[^3](ANNOTATIONS.md#a3) Those stones are the oldest memory of my life. They bore angles that the eye could not follow to their ends, and the figure of a thing with a pulpy, tentacled head squatting upon a pedestal of glyphs; and the old men of the isle said that they came up from a city sunk far out beyond the reefs, where One slept who would wake when the stars came right.[^4](ANNOTATIONS.md#a4) I believed them. More than that, I loved what they told me, as a boy loves a storm seen from a window; and when I was grown I gave my life to it.

I will not describe the rites of that priesthood, nor the years by which I rose through it. It is enough to say that I learned the tongue the stones were written in, and the black arts that the Sleeper sends in dreams to those who listen; and that by my fortieth year there was no voice on that coast which chanted the old words—*Ph'nglui mglw'nafh Cthulhu R'lyeh wgah'nagl fhtagn*—with more authority than mine.[^5](ANNOTATIONS.md#a5) And it is enough to say that on the Night of the Rising Star, when the whole of our congregation stood in the ring of standing stones upon the outermost reef, and the sea before us had begun to boil and bulge with something rising, I was the one who held the knife.

The warrior came out of the sea-mist alone.[^6](ANNOTATIONS.md#a6) I have tried for half a lifetime to recall the face, and cannot; I recall only the eyes, which were steady, and a blade that did not shine as steel shines, but seemed rather a place where the green phosphorescence of the reef could not go. Our circle broke as dry leaves break before a wind. Within a few heartbeats the chant had faltered into screaming, the rising thing had sunk back with a sound like a great ship foundering, and I lay on the wet stone with that strange edge laid cold against my throat.

I waited for the stroke. It did not come. The warrior looked at me a long while, as one looks at a thing one has known before, and said only: "Not yet. You will be needed." Then the blade was withdrawn, and the figure walked back into the mist by the way it had come; and when the sun rose there was no print upon the sand but my own, and no sign of that sword at all.[^7](ANNOTATIONS.md#a7)

What followed is the history of my penance, and it is long. I burned my vestments. I studied for thirty years what I had served, that I might learn how it is fought; and I raised upon the Drowned Coast a ward of signs and watchfulness that holds the Sleeper's dreams below the waves, so that they do not wash inland into the sleep of honest folk. That ward is the reason I could not go to war beside my pupil. My body must sit in trance within its circle for all but a few nights of any year; and if I leave it longer, the dreams of R'lyeh come up the rivers like a tide.[^8](ANNOTATIONS.md#a8)

### II. The Dreamer in the Shop

Arkham in those days was a walled market-town of steep gambrel roofs and leaning gables at the falls of the Miskatonic, where the barges unloaded salt and iron below the bridge and the bells of the Collegium rang the hours over the river.[^9](ANNOTATIONS.md#a9) On the street that climbs from the wharves toward the old burying-ground stood the shop of Abner Pell, chandler and general merchant, who sold candles and rope and lamp-oil and seed-corn and whatever else a valley household might lack; and in that shop, sweeping the boards and minding the counter and running errands up and down the hill, worked the helper who is the subject of this account.[^10](ANNOTATIONS.md#a10)

There was nothing remarkable in my pupil's waking life, and I record this with care, because the minstrels will wish to make of it a hidden prince or a foundling of the stars. My pupil was an ordinary young person of the valley, liked by the neighbours, scolded by Pell, and clever with the ledger. Only in sleep was there anything strange. Since earliest childhood my pupil had dreamed, night upon night, of a city built upon the clouds: of pink marble terraces where the west wind flows into the sky, of carven towers and galleys moored to the sunset, of a harbour without water.[^11](ANNOTATIONS.md#a11) Those dreams were the thing I had come so far to find.

I came in the first week of autumn, when strange lights had already begun to be seen beyond the clouds at dusk, and the farmers of the upper valley spoke of stars that went out and did not return.[^12](ANNOTATIONS.md#a12) I had spent two nights of my ward upon the road, and I could ill spare a third. Yet when I stepped into Pell's shop I forgot for a moment all urgency; for above the counter, hung on two iron pegs among the coils of rope and the strings of onions, was a sword whose blade did not shine as steel shines.

"Old thing," said Pell, seeing me look. "Came up in a fisherman's net, years back. Nobody will buy it. Nobody can sharpen it." I did not tell him what I knew. I asked instead to speak with his helper, and I asked my question before my pupil could say a word: "Tell me of the city in the clouds."

<!-- beat id="p1-archsage-arrives" seed="I did not trust the dreaming alone." -->

I did not trust the dreaming alone. I had brought with me, stoppered in a jar of thick green glass, a thing I had taken from a gate that opened briefly on the coast that summer: a sliver of the sky-folk's substance no larger than a hand, of no colour that has a name, which crawled continually against the glass as though the glass were not there.[^13](ANNOTATIONS.md#a13) Strong men have fainted at the sight of it; one of my own acolytes lost the use of speech for a month. I set it upon Pell's counter and bade my pupil look. My pupil looked—and paled, and trembled, and did not look away. Then I knew that the dreams had done their work; that a mind which had half-lived for years in the Dreamlands had grown a kind of callus against the things that dwell there, and could bear to see what must be seen.

I spoke then of what was coming. I said that the horror which had shaped my youth on the Drowned Coast was not alone in the universe; that beyond the Wall of Sleep lies the vast country of dream; and that in the sky of that country, where Serannian once floated pink above the Cerenerian Sea, an enemy had gathered which meant to come through.[^14](ANNOTATIONS.md#a14) I said that the gates would open soon, and that steel could kill what came out of them, but that only one who could stand at a gate's mouth without madness could close it. And before I left—for I had to leave that night, and the ward was already crying out to me across the leagues—I set a thread of my own mind within my pupil's, so that we might speak across any distance as men speak across a table.[^15](ANNOTATIONS.md#a15) It was the last time for many months that I saw my pupil's face with my own eyes.

### III. The Opening of the Sky

The lessons began the next morning, and were meant to last a year.[^16](ANNOTATIONS.md#a16) Captain Mercy Thorne of the Arkham Watch, a grey and patient woman who had held the bridge against river-pirates in her youth, taught my pupil the uses of the blade and the crossbow, and how to give ground without losing it. I taught by night, through the thread between our minds, the only thing that mattered more: the Sign.[^17](ANNOTATIONS.md#a17)

It is not the Elder Sign of the old books, though it is kin to it. It is a shape made with the hand and the will together, which I learned in my priesthood as a means of *opening*, and spent thirty years learning to turn inside out. Made at the mouth of one of the sky-folk's gates, with the whole strength of a steady mind behind it, it draws the edges of the wound together as a surgeon's stitch draws flesh; and every gate so closed leaves the Wall of Sleep a little stronger than before. I taught it hastily, in a single night, because I had begun to feel through the thread a pressure in the sky above Arkham, like the stillness before thunder. "It is too early," I said; and then, because I would not lie to my pupil, "but it will have to be enough."

<!-- beat id="p1-first-incursion" seed="The sky above the old burying-ground did not tear as cloth tears;" -->

The sky above the old burying-ground did not tear as cloth tears; it was *unwritten*, as though some hand had scraped away a line of the world's text and left the vellum bare beneath. Through that pale gap came first a sound of fluttering, and then the zoogs—little furtive brown things out of the Enchanted Wood, in such numbers as no traveller of dream has ever recorded—and behind them, stepping down out of the air as men step from a boat, the horned raiders of Leng with their curved blades and their hooded faces.[^18](ANNOTATIONS.md#a18) They came over the graveyard wall and into the steep streets, and Arkham, which had gone to bed beneath an ordinary moon, woke screaming.

I watched it through my pupil's eyes. I saw the Watch form up at the head of the hill under Captain Thorne, and break, and form again. I saw Abner Pell come out of his shop with a lamp in one hand and a cudgel in the other, and go down under a raider's blade on his own doorstep.[^19](ANNOTATIONS.md#a19) And I saw my pupil—a chandler's helper of a few weeks' training, armed with a borrowed sword and a single lesson in the Sign—climb the hill against the flood of that invasion, through the burying-ground among the tilted slates, until the gate itself hung overhead like a hole in the night.

What it is to stand at such a gate I know better than any man living, and I will not pretend that my pupil did it without fear. The mind is pulled toward the opening as water toward a drain; one hears voices in it, speaking one's own name in the tongue of the stones; and the things that come out of it pass so near that one smells upon them the cold of the gulfs. My pupil stood there, raised the hand, and made the Sign as I had taught it—once, and failed, and was driven to the ground; twice, and held; and the third time the edges of the unwritten sky crept together, and shuddered, and were whole, and the last raider caught on our side of the Wall turned and fled into the dark.[^20](ANNOTATIONS.md#a20)

Pell lived. He lost the use of his left arm, and his shop lost its windows and half its stock; and the next morning, when they carried him in to the Collegium's infirmary, he asked only whether anyone had thought to put up the shutters.

### IV. The Stone Circle above Dunwich

The lessons resumed, as I had sworn they would; but they were never again so orderly, nor so long.[^21](ANNOTATIONS.md#a21) Magister Henry Armitage, keeper of the Collegium's library, taught my pupil the lesser wards and the draughts that steady a shaken mind; and between lessons the valley itself became a school harsher than either of us. There were bandits on the Aylesbury Pike, grown bold now that the Watch was busy elsewhere; wolves in the upper woods that were only wolves; old houses in Kingsport and old tombs in the Arkham hills where the quieter terrors of our own world had waited a long while for someone to disturb them.[^22](ANNOTATIONS.md#a22) My pupil went into all of these, and came out of most of them stronger, and of a few of them sadder; and I, listening through the thread from my tower, began to understand that I was no longer teaching a helper but advising a captain.

The second gate opened in the round hills above Dunwich, within one of those circles of standing stones that crown their summits and that the hill-folk will not visit after dark.[^23](ANNOTATIONS.md#a23) Out of it came ghasts—those pallid things of the under-places that leap on long hind legs and die in the light, and therefore came by night—and with them, for the first time, men. They were men of our own valley in hooded robes, who had built fires about the stones to feed the gate; and their chant was one I knew, for I had written half its verses myself.

At their head was the one I had once called my apprentice.[^24](ANNOTATIONS.md#a24) He had been a boy when I renounced the priesthood, and I had believed him scattered with the rest; but he had kept the old faith in secret all those years, and had found new masters for it. He knew of the thread between my pupil's mind and mine, and he spoke through my pupil's ears to me. "The sky-folk have promised," he said, "that when the Wall of Sleep falls, the Sleeper will wake. You chose the wrong side of the gate, master. I have chosen the right one." Then the torches came up the hill—for my pupil had taught the Dunwich farmers what a ghast fears—and the gate was sealed; and he went through it before it closed, laughing.

From that night we knew that the gates were not merely forced from without. They were being opened from within.

### V. The Black Tongue

In the long weeks after Dunwich I did the thing I had sworn I would never do: I taught my pupil a working of the enemy's own art.[^25](ANNOTATIONS.md#a25) It was not a great working, as such things go—a word of command that the creatures of the sky-cities are bred to obey, spoken in the tongue of the stones—but it is a weapon that wounds the hand that holds it; and as I taught it I felt the old wet voice rising underneath my own, as it had not risen in thirty years. I believe my pupil heard it too. I know that my pupil used that word only when nothing else would serve, and that each time it was used, the dreams of the cloud-city came that night a little darker; and I record here, because no one else will, that it was a greater courage to refuse that power a hundred times than to use it once.

### VI. The Galley from the Clouds

Kingsport is an old port clinging beneath great cliffs to the south of Arkham, and on the highest of those cliffs, where the mists come up from the sea, there stands a single ancient house to which no road has ever been made.[^26](ANNOTATIONS.md#a26) The fishermen say that its tenant entertains strange guests, and that on some nights the windows of that house look out upon things that are not the sea. It was over Kingsport that the sky-folk made their last and greatest stroke against the valley, and it was in the shadow of that house that my pupil met it.

<!-- beat id="p1-galley" seed="The galley came down out of the sea-mist as a hawk comes down," -->

The galley came down out of the sea-mist as a hawk comes down, black-hulled and black-sailed, rowed by things that were not men, and trailing behind it across the sky a line of opening gates as a ship trails a wake.[^27](ANNOTATIONS.md#a27) Out of it poured the moon-beasts—great greyish-white slippery things that swell and shrink at will, eyeless as toads, with a quivering mass of short pink feelers where a face should be—and their Leng-slaves with them, down upon the harbour and the narrow streets; and three gates stood open at once above the town, so that by the end of the first hour there were dead in every lane.

I have heard the minstrels' version of that night, and I will not correct its ornaments, for most of them are true. My pupil did go from gate to gate along the roofs of Kingsport; Captain Thorne did hold the harbour-stairs with forty of the Watch; the fishermen did set their own boats afire and push them against the galley's hull. What the minstrels do not know is that in the moment before the third gate closed, the master of that expedition came down to finish the matter in person: a moon-beast overseer mounted upon a Shantak, one of those scaly birds larger than elephants with heads like a horse's, which my pupil fought upon the bare cliff-top beneath the windows of the high house while the galley burned below.[^28](ANNOTATIONS.md#a28)

The Shantak fell into the sea. The overseer did not long survive it. And when it was over, and my pupil stood alone upon the cliff with the dawn coming up out of the ocean, one gate remained—far out beyond the harbour mouth, vaster than all the others together, a pale wound upon the sky that did not answer to the Sign.[^29](ANNOTATIONS.md#a29) Through that opening, for the space of a breath, I saw with my pupil's eyes a city of terraces and towers floating upon the clouds, exactly as my pupil had dreamed it since childhood; save that its marble was no longer pink, but black.

## Part II: The Dreamlands

### VII. The Seventy Steps

A gate that cannot be closed from this side must be closed from the other; and no waking body can pass through such a wound and live.[^31](ANNOTATIONS.md#a31) It was the tenant of the high house above Kingsport who showed us the way, for he was a dreamer of the old kind, and his house a place where the roads of sleep come near the earth. My pupil slept there, in a chamber whose windows looked on fog, and in light slumber descended, as other dreamers have descended before, the seventy steps to the cavern of flame, where the bearded priests Nasht and Kaman-Thah keep the threshold of dream.[^32](ANNOTATIONS.md#a32) They had seen many dreamers pass. They had not seen one before who carried a sword out of the waking world; for my pupil, at my bidding, had taken down from Pell's pegs the blade that came out of the sea, and it went into dream as easily as a thought.[^33](ANNOTATIONS.md#a33) The priests looked long at it and said nothing; and then they opened the way to the seven hundred steps and the Gate of Deeper Slumber, beyond which lies the Enchanted Wood.

### VIII. The Wood, the Cats, and the Port

The zoogs that had swarmed over Arkham's burying-ground were not, it proved, the sky-folk's allies, but their pressed servants: the moon-beasts had come into the Enchanted Wood with fire, and taken every third zoog as a hostage against the rest.[^34](ANNOTATIONS.md#a34) My pupil, who had killed many zoogs and might well have hated them, chose instead to free the hostages from a moon-beast camp in the wood; and the zoogs, who are curious creatures and very old, remember a kindness as long as they remember a wrong. Their council gave my pupil passage and the knowledge of the paths; and so my pupil came to Ulthar beyond the river Skai, where no man may kill a cat.

The cats of Ulthar had their own quarrel with the moon-beasts, older than ours, and they heard my pupil's account with that grave courtesy which cats reserve for those who speak their language properly.[^35](ANNOTATIONS.md#a35) They promised nothing. But the old cat who sleeps upon the temple steps went that night to the rooftops and spoke for a long time to the moon; and in the morning there was a different quality in the way the cats of Ulthar watched the sky. From Ulthar my pupil went down to Dylath-Leen, where the black galleys had traded their rubies in the time of Randolph Carter, and found that they traded there again; and that the hooded factor who dealt for them was my old apprentice, who escaped by water before my pupil reached the quay.

### IX. The King Without a Cloud

Kuranes, King of Ooth-Nargai, was once a dreamer of London, and is now a monarch of dream who held his court alternately in Celephaïs and in cloud-fashioned Serannian; but when my pupil came to him he held it only in Celephaïs, for Serannian had fallen.[^36](ANNOTATIONS.md#a36) He told the story as a man tells of a death in his family. The moon-beasts had come out of the west on galleys that sailed upon the air, on a night without moon; his guards had been overwhelmed upon the terraces; and the city's marble, which had been pink with the light of a sunset that never ended, had gone black within a week, as though the enemy's mere presence were a kind of rot. Kuranes had been carried out by his own servants in a fishing boat. He had not slept well since.

A guest sat at Kuranes's table that night whose presence, in the age of the world where my pupil was born, ought to have been impossible: a tired, gentle gentleman of a far later century named Randolph Carter, for whom the Dreamlands are not a place but a home, and in whose sleep the years of the waking world run in no particular order.[^37](ANNOTATIONS.md#a37) Carter listened to all of it and said little; but in the morning he put into my pupil's hand a great key of tarnished silver, and said that it had once taken him back to his own boyhood, and that a time might come when it would be needed to go somewhere stranger.[^38](ANNOTATIONS.md#a38)

It was Kuranes who spoke of the Sky Road. "There was a way," he said, "for those who did not sail: a working of the old dream-kings, that would take one walking up the light into Serannian. The moon-beasts took the tablet that held it when they took my city, and dropped it, I am told, from the galley that carried it away. Whatever falls in the Dreamlands falls at last into the Underworld; and whatever falls into the Underworld, the ghouls keep."[^39](ANNOTATIONS.md#a39)

### X. Ngranek, and the Vale of Pnath

To reach the Underworld my pupil went the way that Carter once went, though for a different reason: to the isle of Oriab and the mountain Ngranek, on whose far side the gods carved their own likeness, and among whose peaks the night-gaunts roost.[^40](ANNOTATIONS.md#a40) Night-gaunts are faceless, rubbery, horned and winged, and they serve neither the moon-beasts nor Nyarlathotep, but only hoary Nodens, Lord of the Great Abyss; and my pupil, climbing the lava slopes of Ngranek by night, did not try to fight them when they came. My pupil let them take hold; and they bore their burden as they bear all burdens, in silence and with an unpleasant tickling, down into the black Vale of Pnath, where the bones of the ages are heaped; and there they let my pupil fall.

### XI. The Underworld

I will be brief about the Underworld, because my pupil has asked me to be.[^41](ANNOTATIONS.md#a41) It is enough to say that the ghouls of that place are not the monsters of our graveyards, or not only so; that their chief among those who dwell nearest the waking world is a painter of Boston named Richard Pickman, who went down to them of his own will in a century not yet arrived; and that Pickman, who has a painter's eye for a face, knew my pupil at once for an ally.[^42](ANNOTATIONS.md#a42)

<!-- beat id="p2-underworld-war" seed="Nine nights the war went on in the dark beneath the earth," -->

The ghouls did keep the tablet of the Sky Road, in a vault of fallen things beside the Vaults of Zin, and they would give it for a price: aid against the gugs, those huge and silent things whose city of cyclopean round towers rises beneath the earth, and against the ghasts that hunt both. My pupil paid that price in full, in a war in the dark that I watched through the thread for nine nights without sleeping; and when the ghouls brought the tablet out, Pickman added to it a promise of his own. "When you go up," he said, "we will come with you."

### XII. Leng and the Yellow Mask

North of the Underworld's exits, across the cold desert plateau of Leng, there stands a prehistoric stone monastery wherein dwells alone the high-priest not to be described, which wears a yellow silken mask over its face.[^43](ANNOTATIONS.md#a43) My apprentice had gone there. I do not know what promises he had been made; I know only what my pupil found, at the end of a long and dreadful passage painted with the history of Leng, in the chamber where the high-priest sat upon its golden throne. The robes were the apprentice's; and the hand that came out of the robes to make the old sign of our priesthood was his hand, or had been. But when my pupil struck the yellow mask away there was nothing behind it but a darkness that laughed with another's voice; and what had been my apprentice was gone long before, consumed by the thing he had chosen to serve.[^44](ANNOTATIONS.md#a44)

My pupil did not stay long to mourn him, and neither did I, though I had loved him once as a son. In Inganok, the onyx city beyond Leng, whose grave people quarry stone for the gods and know the road to Kadath, my pupil read at last the tablet of the Sky Road, and learned it.[^45](ANNOTATIONS.md#a45)

### XIII. The Sky Road

<!-- beat id="p2-sky-road" seed="Then the light itself became a stair," -->

Then the light itself became a stair, and my pupil walked up it into the sky. I will not pretend to describe that ascent, for the tablet's working does not take the body upward through any space that I can name; it takes the dreamer *along* the direction in which Serannian lies, which is neither up nor west, but sideways from both. What I can describe is what followed; for every soul in the Dreamlands who looked up that night saw it.[^46](ANNOTATIONS.md#a46)

The cats of Ulthar came first, as the old cat had promised without promising. They leapt from the rooftops of Ulthar and Nir and Hatheg to the moon, as the cats of dream have always been able to do, in such numbers that the moon's face was dark with them, and fell upon the moon-beasts' towers there with a shrieking that was heard in the waking world as a strange wind.[^47](ANNOTATIONS.md#a47) The ghouls came up out of the earth behind Pickman, riding upon night-gaunts whom Nodens had lent them. And my pupil walked the Sky Road into fallen Serannian, through black terraces and silent squares, to the great gate that hung above its harbour—the far side of the wound that had opened over Kingsport—and made the Sign.

It took a night and a day. I held the thread open for every hour of it, though it cost my ward and very nearly my life; and I know that more than once my pupil's mind went down into that opening as a swimmer goes down in a whirlpool, and came up only because of a voice. Not mine. It was Abner Pell's, grumbling about the shutters; it was Captain Thorne's, ordering the line to hold; it was the bells of the Collegium ringing the hours over the river.[^48](ANNOTATIONS.md#a48) When the gate closed, the black went out of Serannian's marble as night goes out of the sky, beginning at the highest tower; and by the second dawn the city stood pink upon the clouds again, and Kuranes was weeping in its harbour.

### XIV. Kadath

There remained the one who had sent them.[^49](ANNOTATIONS.md#a49) Unknown Kadath stands in the cold waste beyond Leng, where the gods of earth dwell in an onyx castle upon a mountain that touches the stars; and there my pupil went by the Sky Road, alone, for no cat or ghoul or night-gaunt would go nearer to that castle than the foothills.

<!-- beat id="p2-kadath" seed="He was courteous. He was always courteous." -->

Nyarlathotep received my pupil in the great hall of the onyx castle, as he once received Randolph Carter: a tall, slim figure with the young face of an antique Pharaoh, gay with prismatic robes and crowned with a golden pshent that glowed with inherent light.[^50](ANNOTATIONS.md#a50) He was courteous. He was always courteous. He praised my pupil's courage and condoled with my pupil's losses; and then he offered what he offers every dreamer who comes so far: the city of one's dreams, forever. "You have saved Serannian," he said. "Why not keep it? Why go back to a shop in a little walled town, to sweep boards and count candles and grow old? Stay. Be its queen, its king, its saint. I will send you there upon a Shantak, and you need never wake."[^51](ANNOTATIONS.md#a51)

That was the snare that once nearly took Randolph Carter, and Carter escaped it only by remembering at the last the roofs of the town where he had been a boy. My pupil escaped it in the same way, and in a different one.[^52](ANNOTATIONS.md#a52) My pupil did remember Arkham—the gambrel roofs, the barges below the bridge, Pell's voice—but it was not memory that answered Nyarlathotep. It was a choice. "Serannian was never mine," said my pupil. "I only dreamed it. I *live* in Arkham." And the Crawling Chaos, who has never in all the aeons understood why such an answer should be made, laughed his terrible laugh, and opened a door in the air behind my pupil, and pushed.

### XV. The Night of the Rising Star

I come now to the part of this account which I have been afraid to write, because it concerns myself, and because I did not understand it until it was told to me.[^53](ANNOTATIONS.md#a53)

The door through which the Crawling Chaos thrust my pupil opened not upon a place but upon a time. It was meant for a trap and a jest together: a door into the waking world thirty-odd years in the past, upon the outermost reef of the Drowned Coast, on the Night of the Rising Star, where a priest of Cthulhu stood in a ring of stones with the knife in his hand and the sea before him bulging with what was rising. If my pupil did nothing, the rite would be finished and the Sleeper would wake. If my pupil killed that priest—as any reasonable person would—there would be no one, thirty years later, to build the ward, nor to see the invasion coming, nor to find a chandler's helper in Arkham and teach the Sign; and the sky-cities would have their way after all. Nyarlathotep is fond of such arrangements.

My pupil came out of the sea-mist alone, with the silver key of Randolph Carter in one hand and the sword out of Pell's shop in the other.[^54](ANNOTATIONS.md#a54) The circle broke. The rising thing sank back. The priest lay on the wet stone with that strange edge against his throat, and waited for the stroke; and my pupil, looking down at a face that would one day be old and tired and dear, said the only thing that could be said: "Not yet. You will be needed." Then my pupil turned the silver key, and stepped back into the mist, and left the sword standing in the sand for the tide to carry wherever it would.

I have since learned that a fisherman of the Miskatonic mouth drew it up in his net some years afterward, and sold it for a few coppers to a chandler of Arkham named Pell, who hung it over his counter on two iron pegs because nobody would buy it.[^55](ANNOTATIONS.md#a55)

## Epilogue: The Last Door

The sky over the valley is whole again, and the stars that went out have returned.[^56](ANNOTATIONS.md#a56) The Wall of Sleep is stronger than it has been in my lifetime; Serannian floats pink above the Cerenerian Sea; and in Arkham the shop of Abner Pell has new windows, of which its owner complains daily. My pupil comes and goes there, between errands of a kind that no chandler ever sent a helper upon.

But I am old, and my ward is failing with me, and in these last months I have felt beneath the Drowned Coast a stirring that I have not felt since I was a priest. The Sleeper in R'lyeh has noticed us. The sky-cities were never his; but he too was promised the fall of the Wall, and he too is patient; and when I am gone there will be nothing between his dreams and the sleep of the valley but the Sign, and the one who carries it.[^57](ANNOTATIONS.md#a57)

I have told my pupil this. I have told my pupil, too, the way to R'lyeh, and the shape of the last door, and what I believe it will cost to close it. My pupil listened as I once listened on the outermost reef, with steady eyes, and said: "Then I will need my sword." It hangs over Pell's counter still. My pupil goes to fetch it in the morning.

*Here the Archsage's manuscript ends. Below it, in a different and firmer hand, one line more is written:*

*The door is shut.*[^58](ANNOTATIONS.md#a58)
